SmartBrush.
Innovation project

Technology for a safer future.

The Smart Brush is an innovative, ergonomic robotic tool designed to safeguard delicate cultural heritage artifacts during restoration and cleaning. By providing real-time visual, acoustic, and haptic pressure feedback, it prevents accidental breakage and empowers conservators, students, and individuals with impaired motor control.

Our idea

A smart restoration brush equipped with Force Sensitive Resistors (FSR) that continuously monitors applied force, helping users maintain optimal pressure on delicate historical surfaces.

Development

Evolved across five iterations—from non-programmable analog prototypes (Models 1 & 2) to micro-controlled architectures featuring OLED/7-segment displays and Bluetooth connectivity (Models 3, 4-CH, and 4-ESP).

Our impact

Extensively field-tested with museum archaeologists, forensic specialists, and professional art restorers. Reduces artifact damage without automating or replacing human craftsmanship.

Why SmartBrush matters

A restoration brush may look like a simple hand tool, but the pressure applied to its tip can determine whether a fragile surface remains intact. SmartBrush adds an electronic layer of awareness to a familiar manual instrument. It does not take control away from the specialist; it gives the specialist additional information at the moment it is needed.

Protecting fragile surfaces

During archaeological cleaning, even a small and repeated increase in force can loosen particles, scratch a surface or damage a delicate structure. SmartBrush is intended to make changes in applied pressure easier to notice before they become a problem.

Supporting the user

Visual, sound and vibration feedback offer several ways to communicate the measured pressure. This is useful when the user is concentrating on a small detail and cannot continuously look at a display.

Designed to evolve

The project has been developed through successive prototypes. Each version provided an opportunity to evaluate the sensing method, electronics, feedback, enclosure and practical usability.

From an ordinary brush to a smart instrument

The central idea is to preserve the natural movement and working method of a traditional brush while adding measurement and feedback. The user still decides how to move, where to clean and how much pressure is appropriate. SmartBrush simply measures the interaction and warns when the configured limit is approached or exceeded.

The project brings together mechanical design, electronics, embedded programming and practical testing. Its development is therefore not limited to building a circuit: the complete tool must be comfortable to hold, understandable to operate, serviceable and adaptable to different working conditions.

The idea

The project.

Restoring delicate objects—such as ancient bones, paper, glass, or ceramics—demands fine pressure control. The Smart Brush combines modular 3D-printed housings, custom PCBs, FSR sensing, and real-time alerts to solve this challenge.

Problem

Fragile artifacts are easily damaged during cleaning. Novice conservators, students, or individuals with physical hand injuries or motor impairments frequently struggle with pressure control, risking irreparable loss.

Solution

The Smart Brush measures force in real time. If applied pressure exceeds predefined thresholds, it alerts the user instantly via multi-color RGB LEDs, acoustic buzzers, and subtle haptic vibration motor feedback.

Powered by Force Sensitive Resistors (FSR), ESP32-WROOM-32 / CH32V203C8T6 microcontrollers, custom PCBs, 3D TinkerCAD housings, interchangeable tips, and AI-assisted sliding-window noise filtering algorithms.

How the system works

SmartBrush follows a continuous measurement-and-feedback cycle. The sensor responds to pressure, the microcontroller reads and processes the signal, and the device communicates the result through the selected feedback outputs.

1. Detect

The Force Sensitive Resistor (FSR) changes its electrical resistance when force is applied. Connected as part of a voltage divider, this change can be measured as an analogue voltage by the microcontroller's ADC input.

2. Process

The controller samples the sensor and evaluates the readings against a configured threshold. A moving-average filter can reduce short fluctuations, making the displayed value and warning behaviour more stable.

3. Inform

When the measured pressure reaches a warning region, the device can use an RGB indicator, buzzer and vibration motor. Different feedback channels make the warning noticeable without requiring the user to stop and inspect the electronics.

Pressure sensing and calibration

FSR sensors are useful for detecting changes in applied force, but they are not precision laboratory force gauges. Their response can vary with the sensor, mounting method, contact area, loading history and the surface beneath the sensor. For that reason, SmartBrush uses relative readings and configurable thresholds rather than claiming a universally accurate force value.

Before practical use, the sensor should be tested in the final mechanical assembly. The threshold should be selected for the intended brush tip and task, and checked against a safe working procedure. A setting suitable for one material or object may not be suitable for another.

Feedback designed for focused work

Visual feedback provides an immediate status indication. Acoustic feedback can attract attention when the user is not looking at the device, while haptic feedback can be felt through the handle. Combining these methods allows the user to choose a warning style that suits the environment. For example, a quiet museum workspace may require a lower buzzer volume and greater reliance on visual or vibration feedback.

Modular construction

The electronics and enclosure are designed as parts of one handheld system. A custom PCB helps organise connections and reduce loose wiring, while a 3D-printed housing protects the components and provides a form that can be held like a conventional brush. The modular approach also makes it easier to revise the enclosure, replace a component or test a different controller.

Current development goals

  • Improve the mechanical attachment between the sensor and the brush contact area.
  • Make sensor calibration repeatable between prototypes.
  • Evaluate warning thresholds with different brush tips and working surfaces.
  • Improve battery monitoring and power consumption.
  • Keep the control interface simple and make wireless configuration optional.

Signal Processing & Software

FSR sensor signals are sampled via ADC and processed using a 10-sample sliding-window moving-average filter (`readFSR()`). This noise-filtering algorithm eliminates false spikes caused by high sensor sensitivity. Digital debouncing (`bot()`) handles threshold adjustments.

Electronics architecture

The electronics are organised around four functional blocks: sensing, processing, feedback and power. The FSR and its measurement circuit provide the input signal. The microcontroller reads the signal and executes the control logic. Output pins drive the indicator, buzzer and vibration motor through suitable driver circuits where required. The battery and charging or regulation circuitry supply the system.

Separating these functions makes the design easier to test. The sensor can be checked independently from the display or warning outputs, and each controller version can reuse the same general concept while adapting pin assignments and peripheral capabilities.

Software structure

The embedded program repeatedly reads the analogue sensor input, filters the raw samples, compares the processed value with the selected threshold and updates the outputs. Keeping measurement, filtering, decision-making and output control as separate logical steps makes the program easier to maintain and debug.

Sampling

The ADC converts the sensor voltage into a numerical reading. Repeated samples allow the software to observe how the signal changes while the brush is in contact with a surface.

Filtering

A 10-sample moving average, as used in the described implementation, smooths rapid fluctuations. Filtering can improve stability, although it also introduces a small delay that should be considered during calibration.

Threshold logic

The processed value is compared with a configurable limit. The output state is then updated to communicate whether the measured pressure is within the selected range or requires attention.

Wireless interface and configuration

The ESP32-WROOM-32 version includes Bluetooth Low Energy communication. A compatible browser can connect to the device, request sensor data and send supported configuration commands. The MyDevice page provides controls for reading the sensor state, changing buzzer volume, selecting sensitivity and transferring a firmware file.

Wireless communication is intended for configuration and monitoring; it does not replace the local pressure-warning function. The device should continue to provide its core feedback directly, without depending on a phone or an internet connection.

Testing and validation

Testing should include sensor response at different pressure levels, repeatability after repeated loading, stability when the handle is moved, warning response time, battery behaviour and the reliability of the wireless connection. The enclosure should also be checked for comfort, access to controls and protection of the electronic assembly.

Field observations and prototype demonstrations are valuable for identifying practical issues, but they should not be confused with formal certification or a controlled scientific performance study. Any performance claim should be supported by a documented test method and recorded results.

Prototype comparison

The first analog versions explored the basic pressure-detection principle with a comparator and simple alerts. Later versions introduced programmable control, more flexible thresholds and additional feedback. The CH32V203C8T6 variant focuses on a compact controller implementation, while the ESP32-WROOM-32 variant adds BLE communication and remote configuration.

What determines the cost?

The final cost depends on the selected controller, sensor mounting, battery and charging circuit, PCB production method, enclosure material and the number of prototypes produced. A one-off prototype is usually more expensive per unit than a small production batch because development and setup costs are spread across fewer devices.

Electronic components

The electronics include the FSR sensor, microcontroller, passive components, RGB indicator, buzzer, vibration motor and power-management parts. The ESP32 version also uses the controller's built-in Bluetooth capability, avoiding the need for a separate wireless module.

PCB and assembly

A custom printed circuit board can make the device more compact and reliable than a prototype assembled with many loose wires. The total depends on board dimensions, copper layers, order quantity and whether assembly is performed manually or by a manufacturer.

Housing and accessories

The enclosure can be produced with common 3D-printing materials such as PLA or PETG. Print time, material usage, supports, finishing and interchangeable brush attachments all contribute to the final cost.

Power system

A rechargeable lithium battery, charging connector, protection and voltage regulation are required for portable operation. Battery capacity should be selected after measuring the actual current consumption of the finished prototype.

Two controller directions

The Model 4-CH concept uses the CH32V203C8T6 and is intended as a cost-conscious controller option. The Model 4-ESP uses the ESP32-WROOM-32 and adds Bluetooth Low Energy functionality for telemetry and configuration. The best choice depends on whether wireless control is required for the intended use.

Cost planning for future versions

A realistic budget should distinguish between development expenses and repeatable unit costs. Development expenses include design time, test materials, failed prints and prototype revisions. Unit costs include the parts and manufacturing steps needed for each finished device. Keeping these categories separate makes it easier to estimate the cost of a future small production run.

At this stage, the website does not publish fixed prices because component suppliers, order quantities and the final bill of materials may change. A detailed cost table should be completed using verified purchase prices and the exact hardware configuration.

The development process

The gallery documents more than the finished appearance of the device. It should show how the idea developed, what was tested and how the mechanical and electronic parts were gradually brought together.

Early experiments

The first prototypes were used to investigate the relationship between pressure and the sensor output. Simple analogue circuits made it possible to test the basic warning principle before adding more complex software.

Electronics integration

Later prototypes brought the sensor, controller and feedback components into one system. These stages helped evaluate wiring, component placement, power requirements and the practical size of the electronics.

Enclosure development
