SmartBrush.
Innovation project

Technology for a safer future.

The Smart Brush is an innovative, ergonomic robotic tool designed to safeguard delicate cultural heritage artifacts during restoration and cleaning. By providing real-time visual, acoustic, and haptic pressure feedback, it prevents accidental breakage and empowers conservators, students, and individuals with impaired motor control.

Our idea

A smart restoration brush equipped with Force Sensitive Resistors (FSR) that continuously monitors applied force, helping users maintain optimal pressure on delicate historical surfaces.

Development

Evolved across five iterations—from non-programmable analog prototypes (Models 1 & 2) to micro-controlled architectures featuring OLED/7-segment displays and Bluetooth connectivity (Models 3, 4-CH, and 4-ESP).

Our impact

Extensively field-tested with museum archaeologists, forensic specialists, and professional art restorers. Reduces artifact damage without automating or replacing human craftsmanship.

Why SmartBrush matters

A restoration brush may look like a simple hand tool, but the pressure applied to its tip can determine whether a fragile surface remains intact. SmartBrush adds an electronic layer of awareness to a familiar manual instrument. It does not take control away from the specialist; it gives the specialist additional information at the moment it is needed.

Protecting fragile surfaces

During archaeological cleaning, even a small and repeated increase in force can loosen particles, scratch a surface or damage a delicate structure. SmartBrush is intended to make changes in applied pressure easier to notice before they become a problem.

Supporting the user

Visual, sound and vibration feedback offer several ways to communicate the measured pressure. This is useful when the user is concentrating on a small detail and cannot continuously look at a display.

Designed to evolve

The project has been developed through successive prototypes. Each version provided an opportunity to evaluate the sensing method, electronics, feedback, enclosure and practical usability.

From an ordinary brush to a smart instrument

The central idea is to preserve the natural movement and working method of a traditional brush while adding measurement and feedback. The user still decides how to move, where to clean and how much pressure is appropriate. SmartBrush simply measures the interaction and warns when the configured limit is approached or exceeded.

The project brings together mechanical design, electronics, embedded programming and practical testing. Its development is therefore not limited to building a circuit: the complete tool must be comfortable to hold, understandable to operate, serviceable and adaptable to different working conditions.

Who we are

About us.

Our team

A dedicated team of young innovators, engineers, and designers working at the intersection of embedded systems, 3D printing, and cultural heritage preservation.

Our mission

To deliver practical, highly accessible, and low-cost robotic assistive devices that protect irreplaceable historical artifacts and enhance human precision in restoration work.

Our approach

SmartBrush is an interdisciplinary project. It combines practical engineering with an understanding of the careful work carried out by archaeologists, conservators and restorers. The project is guided by a simple principle: technology should support professional judgement, not replace it.

Research and observation

Development begins by considering the conditions in which delicate objects are cleaned. The tool must be useful in close-up work, where the user needs precise hand movements and clear feedback without unnecessary distraction.

Engineering and prototyping

Electronic circuits, sensors, microcontrollers and 3D-printed parts are combined in working prototypes. Testing each version helps identify what should be improved before the next iteration is built.

Accessibility and affordability

The project aims to use accessible components and a modular construction. A practical assistive tool should be maintainable and should not require an expensive or complicated installation to provide its core function.

Who can benefit?

Potential users include museum and archaeological professionals, restoration workshops, students learning conservation techniques and people who may benefit from additional assistance with fine motor control. The exact warning levels and working technique must always be adapted to the object, material and professional procedure.

Our goal is to continue improving the prototype through feedback, repeatable tests and careful documentation. We consider the device a support instrument: the experience and responsibility of the conservator remain essential at every stage of the work.
